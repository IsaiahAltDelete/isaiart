02 SCREEN
The distance between dot centres, measured in source pixels. Everything else is a consequence of this: coarser means fewer, larger dots and less of the picture surviving. Newsprint sat around a tenth of the plate width; a comic screen is finer than that; manga tone finer again. 45° is not a style choice. The eye resolves horizontal and vertical structure far better than diagonal, so a lattice turned to 45° disappears into tone while the same lattice at 0° reads as a grid. In four-colour work this becomes the black angle and the other three are placed around it.
ROUND is the default and steps visibly at 50%, where four dots touch at once. ELLIPTICAL and EUCLIDEAN both exist to remove that step — Euclidean by inverting into a white dot past the midpoint. LINE and CROSS-HATCH stop being print and start being drawing.
SMOOTHING
A halftone is a one-bit image: every point is ink or paper, nothing between. OFF is that image, and it is what you want if the plate is going anywhere near a printer. The others supersample the dot edge so it survives being looked at on a screen at less than 1:1 — at the cost of that many times the render.
03 TONE
SAMPLING
AREA rotates every source pixel into screen space and averages the ones that fall in each cell, so a dot's size is the true mean tone underneath it. That is what a process camera did, and it is why the result still reads correctly when the dots are coarser than the detail.

DETAIL compares the screen against the picture where it stands. Dots deform around edges and keep far more of the drawing, but the tone is only honest where the picture is smooth. Use AREA for photographs and coarse screens, DETAIL for line art and fine ones.
Where the tone stops being a dot and becomes solid. These clip before anything else runs, so a black point of 12% means "anything darker than this prints as flat ink" — which is how inked artwork keeps its blacks instead of dissolving into 95% dots, and how highlights stay bare paper instead of collecting stray specks. Coverage is 1 − linear luminance by default, which is the physically right answer and darker than most filters give you. This bends it. Ink spreads where it meets paper, so a 50% dot prints nearer 60% — and only the midtones move, because a cell with no dot in it has no edge to spread from. Positive simulates the press; negative is the compensation a prepress operator would have applied to cancel it. Screentone came on sheets, and a sheet has exactly one density. Snapping coverage to a ladder of densities is what turns a photograph into something that could plausibly have been cut and laid down by hand. Ink the highlights instead — a negative plate, or white line on a dark field.
04 INK
PLATES
Normally the screen replaces the picture: ink on bare stock, and everything the dots do not cover is paper. Turn this on and the picture becomes the stock — the same dots, in the same places, laid over the original instead of standing in for it. That is how tone was actually applied to artwork: the drawing stays, the screen goes on top of it. How much of what is underneath a dot actually hides it. Not the same control as dot size — coverage decides how much of the surface the ink lands on, this decides how opaque it is once it gets there. At 100% the ink is solid, which over a picture means the dark end goes flat black; pull it back and the dots read as a screen sitting on top.

SECOND INK #0f5fd0
A duotone is one photograph on two plates: the first ink carries the whole range, the second is dropped in below this point to give the shadows a colour the first ink cannot reach. The two screens sit 30° apart so they interleave instead of beating against each other.
Grey component replacement. Wherever all three colours are called for at once they make a muddy grey, so that common amount is pulled out and given to the black plate instead — less ink, cleaner neutrals, and registration errors stop turning grey into a rainbow. At zero there is no black plate at all.
SEPARATION ANGLES
Each plate gets its own angle, 30° apart, because two identical lattices laid over each other beat into a moiré pattern coarse enough to see. Yellow is the exception — it sits 15° from its neighbours, which it can get away with because yellow on white is the hardest ink to see at all.
PAPER #f4f1e8
Export the ink alone, with the paper knocked out to alpha. That is the file you want if the screen is going over something else in an editor rather than being the finished picture.
05 LINEWORK
Screening destroys thin lines: an eyelash a pixel wide becomes a dot the size of a cell, or nothing. This finds the edges first and draws them at full resolution over the screen, the way an inker lays line over tone rather than under it. Off by default — on a photograph it invents outlines that were never drawn. How much local contrast counts as a line. Low picks up texture and noise; high keeps only the drawn edges.
06 OUTPUT
1× is one output pixel per source pixel. Larger renders the same screen onto a bigger sheet, which is the only way to get a clean dot edge out of a fine pitch — at 7px pitch and 1× there are only seven pixels to draw each dot with.
