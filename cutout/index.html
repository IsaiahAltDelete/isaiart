02 ENGINES IDLE
HOW THEY COMBINE
AVERAGE is a weighted vote — the usual answer, and the only mode where the weight sliders do anything. AGREE keeps a pixel only if every enabled engine calls it subject, which is how you get a mask with no false positives and holes everywhere. ANY is the opposite. Click a thumbnail to see one engine on its own.
BORDER PRIOR
—
Learns a five-component colour model from the frame of the picture, scores every pixel against it, then learns a second model from whatever scored worst and takes the posterior between them. That second, self-taught model is what stops a subject that shares a colour family with its background from vanishing.
How much of the frame to hand the second model as training data. Set it near the fraction of the picture the subject actually occupies: too high and the subject model learns background, too low and it starves.
TRUST THESE EDGES
A portrait cropped at the chest has a bottom edge made entirely of subject. Turn that edge off and the model stops being taught that skin is background.
CHROMA KEY
—
Soft keying with two shells. Inside the inner radius a colour is pure background, outside the outer one it is pure subject, and the ramp between them is where hair and motion blur live. Distance is measured in OKLab, so the chroma and lightness axes can be weighted apart.
KEY COLOURS
none — add one
Click a swatch to remove it. Several keys are allowed — a screen with a lit half and a shadowed half is two colours, not one.
Drop the lightness weight towards zero to forgive an unevenly lit screen; raise it to key something that differs from its background only in brightness.
CONNECTEDNESS
—
Segmentation as a walking problem. Background is whatever you can reach from the frame — or from a background scribble — without crossing a strong edge. A wall that fades from white to grey is one long chain of easy steps and comes out whole; a subject behind one hard edge is cut off no matter what colour it is.
BOTTLENECK is scale-free: a wall is a wall however far away it is. But it saturates — once both a foreground and a background scribble can reach a pixel over easy steps, both score ~1 and the comparison between them is noise. ADDITIVE never saturates, so it is the one that works with scribbles on both sides. AUTOMATIC picks additive the moment you draw a foreground mark.
How big a colour step counts as a wall. Lower is stricter.
SALIENCY
—
Three ways of asking "what is unusual here", averaged because they fail differently. It needs no seeds and no assumptions about the frame, which makes it the useful vote when everything else is confused — but it produces a blurry blob, never an edge. Give it a small weight and let something else find the outline.
Natural images have a roughly 1/f amplitude spectrum. Take this picture's log spectrum, subtract the smoothed version of itself, keep the phase and transform back: what is left is whatever this picture has that an average picture does not.
REGION SPILL
—
Cuts the picture into superpixels, then asks of each one: if I flood outwards from here through colour-similar neighbours, how much of what I reach is the frame? A big uniform wall spills onto the frame from everywhere, even in the middle of the picture. A subject leaning against the frame barely spills at all. That distinction is what a plain border model cannot make.
High makes tidy hexagons that ignore the picture; low makes ragged regions that follow every edge. Low is usually right here.
GRAPH CUT
—
GrabCut, in full. Two five-component colour models with real covariances, an eight-connected field whose edge costs are calibrated against this picture's own contrast, and an exact global minimum found by Boykov–Kolmogorov max-flow — not an approximation, the actual cheapest cut. Then it re-fits the models to the result and does it again. It is the slowest engine here and usually the right one.
Feeding it the other engines' consensus is the strongest setting: the cut then has a real opinion to sharpen instead of a rectangle to guess from. Your brush marks are honoured as hard facts in every mode.
It stops early once the labelling stops moving, so a high number costs nothing on an easy picture.
The price of a boundary. High gives a short, clean outline that ignores detail; low lets the cut wander into every crevice and every JPEG artefact.
PLATE DIFFERENCE
—
With a photograph of the empty scene, the subject is simply what changed. Needs a plate loaded in panel 01.
A cast shadow darkens the plate without changing its colour. At zero that is forgiven and the shadow stays behind; at one it is subject like anything else. In between it becomes partial alpha, which is what you want if you are keeping the shadow.
RAW MARKS
—
Your brush strokes, blurred outwards and nothing else. Not much of an engine, but as a voter it lets one stroke overrule everything above it.

03 MARKS 0 STROKES
TOOL
KEEP and DROP paint hard facts that every engine has to honour. You almost never need many — two strokes on a picture that came out wrong is usually the whole job. Hold space to pan while painting; 1–5 pick a tool.
04 SHAPE
Cheap repairs to a mask that is basically right, applied before any matting happens. Nine times out of ten a mask that looks wrong just needs its midtones pushed apart.
At zero nothing is dropped. At one you get the single biggest piece — which is wrong if the subject is a person and the bag they are carrying, so raise it when that is the case.
A gap that never reaches the frame is a hole in the mask, not a gap in the subject. Turn this off for anything you can genuinely see through — a wicker chair, a pair of glasses.
Positive eats into the subject, negative grows it, measured as a true Euclidean distance so corners move at the same speed as flat sides. A pixel of choke hides almost every remaining fringe.
05 MATTE —
Everything above this point produces one bit per pixel. Hair, fur, glass, motion blur and every antialiased edge are fractions, and this is where they are solved for — inside a band straddling the outline, which is the only place the answer was ever in doubt.
The single most important number on this page. Too narrow and hair is cut off at the root; too wide and the solver has to invent alpha for pixels that were never ambiguous. Switch the view to TRIMAP to see exactly what you are asking it to solve.
The regulariser in the matting Laplacian. Small lets alpha follow colour into every crevice and picks up individual hairs; large behaves more like a smooth interpolation. Below about 1e-9 the maths becomes genuinely ill-conditioned on flat regions and the answer degrades — the slider stops before that.
Mean-field inference over a fully connected field: every pixel talks to every other pixel through a Gaussian on position and colour, which becomes tractable because that sum is a blur, and a blur can be done on a grid. Use it when the matte has the right shape but is bleeding across a colour boundary it should have respected. It is a sharpener, not a fixer — it will happily make a confidently wrong mask worse.
06 COLOUR
An edge pixel is a mixture. Composite it somewhere new without undoing the mixture and it carries a rim of its old surroundings — the fringe that makes a cutout look like a cutout. This estimates the background hiding behind the edge by interpolating inwards from the parts of it you can see, then inverts the mixing equation to recover the subject's true colour.
Full strength divides by alpha, which is loudest exactly where alpha is least trustworthy. Back it off if the edge starts to sparkle.
Green bounced off a screen lands on the subject too, and no alpha channel will remove it. This pushes colour off the key's chroma axis without touching anything on the other side of it — in OKLab, so "the green axis" means the same thing in the shadows as in the highlights. Needs a key colour in panel 02.
07 OUTPUT
Only used by the flat-colour option, but worth setting anyway — the preview uses it as the checkerboard alternative when you press the composite key.

READ ME

There is no single algorithm that removes backgrounds. There are a dozen, each of which is the best one for some pictures and useless for others, and the honest version of this tool is one that shows you all of them arguing rather than one that picks for you and hopes. The thumbnails in panel 02 are the whole idea: nine opinions, side by side, with a weight on each.

A working order, if you want one. Load the picture and look at the thumbnails — usually one of them is already nearly right. Turn the others down. If nothing is right, draw one KEEP stroke inside the subject and one DROP stroke outside it; every engine takes those as fact. Then set the band width in panel 05 wide enough to contain the fuzzy part of the edge, pick a solver, and switch the view to ALPHA to see what you actually have.

The parts worth knowing about: the graph cut finds a genuine global optimum rather than a local one, and its max-flow is checked against brute-force minimum cuts. The closed-form matte never builds the matting Laplacian — it only ever needs the product of that matrix with a vector, and that factors into two three-component passes per window, which is why a solve that ought to need hundreds of megabytes finishes in a fraction of a second. And the whole pipeline runs on a downscaled copy, then carries the answer back to full size through the full-resolution photograph, so working at 768 px does not cost you a soft edge at 6000.

Everything here runs on your machine, offline, with one clearly-marked exception: the neural engine, which downloads a model the first time you switch it on and is off until you do.
