02Screen

100 · 75 · 50 · 25 · 10%

The distance between dot centres, measured in source pixels. Everything else is a consequence of this: coarser means fewer, larger dots and less of the picture surviving. Newsprint sat around a tenth of the plate width; a comic screen is finer than that; manga tone finer again.

45° is not a style choice. The eye resolves horizontal and vertical structure far better than diagonal, so a lattice turned to 45° disappears into tone while the same lattice at 0° reads as a grid. In four-colour work this becomes the black angle and the other three are placed around it.

Round is the default and steps visibly at 50%, where four dots touch at once. Elliptical and Euclidean both exist to remove that step — Euclidean by inverting into a white dot past the midpoint. Line and Cross-hatch stop being print and start being drawing.

Smoothing

A halftone is a one-bit image: every point is ink or paper, nothing between. Off is that image, and it is what you want if the plate is going anywhere near a printer. The others supersample the dot edge so it survives being looked at on a screen at less than 1:1 — at the cost of that many times the render.

03Tone

Sampling

Area rotates every source pixel into screen space and averages the ones that fall in each cell, so a dot's size is the true mean tone underneath it — what a process camera did. Detail compares the screen against the picture where it stands: dots deform around edges and keep far more of the drawing, but tone is only honest where the picture is smooth. Area for photographs and coarse screens, Detail for line art and fine ones.

Where the tone stops being a dot and becomes solid. A black point of 12% means "anything darker than this prints as flat ink" — which is how inked artwork keeps its blacks, and how highlights stay bare paper instead of collecting stray specks.

Coverage is 1 − linear luminance by default, which is the physically right answer and darker than most filters give you. This bends it.

Ink spreads where it meets paper, so a 50% dot prints nearer 60% — and only the midtones move. Positive simulates the press; negative is the compensation a prepress operator would have applied.

Screentone came on sheets, and a sheet has exactly one density. Snapping coverage to a ladder of densities turns a photograph into something that could have been cut and laid down by hand.

Ink the highlights instead — a negative plate, or white line on a dark field.

04Ink

Plates

Normally the screen replaces the picture: ink on bare stock. Turn this on and the picture becomes the stock — the same dots laid over the original instead of standing in for it, which is how tone was actually applied to artwork.

How opaque the ink is once it lands — not the same as dot size. At 100% the ink is solid; pull it back and the dots read as a screen sitting on top.

Second ink #0f5fd0

One photograph on two plates: the first ink carries the whole range, the second is dropped in below this point to colour the shadows. The two screens sit 30° apart so they interleave instead of beating.

Grey component replacement: wherever all three colours are called for at once, that common amount moves to the black plate — less ink, cleaner neutrals. At zero there is no black plate at all.

05Linework

Screening destroys thin lines. This finds the edges first and draws them at full resolution over the screen, the way an inker lays line over tone. Off by default — on a photograph it invents outlines that were never drawn.

How much local contrast counts as a line. Low picks up texture and noise; high keeps only the drawn edges.

06Output

1× is one output pixel per source pixel. Larger renders the same screen onto a bigger sheet — the only way to get a clean dot edge out of a fine pitch.

02Marks

Style

Stipple scatters one dot per cell, nudged off the grid so it reads as pen work rather than print. Scribble swaps the dot for a short curved stroke at a random angle. Dot grid holds the dots on the grid. Re-roll reshuffles the randomness without touching anything else.

One mark per cell this many source pixels across. Each cell is averaged over its whole area, not read at one corner.

The widest a dot gets, at full tone. Area tracks darkness, so a 50% grey inks half as much as black — not a quarter.

Above 1 lets the midtones go bare and keeps only the darks; below 1 fills them in.

02Signal

The vaseline. Luma is resolved far more sharply than colour on tape, so this stays gentle — push chroma bleed for the smeared look instead.

Colour smeared sideways while the outlines stay put — the single control that reads most as "video". VHS carried colour at a fraction of the luminance bandwidth, so reds run into their neighbours.

Colour arriving late, so it sits to the right of the object it belongs to.

The bright lip trailing every dark edge — analogue sharpening overshooting. Small amounts do a lot of the period feel.

03Levels

The faded part. Tape never reached black, so shadows sit milky and grey — this is what stops a filter looking like a modern photo with grain on it.

Negative cools the shadows blue, positive pushes them magenta — the tell-tale cast of a warm dub.

04Glow

Highlights blooming into what surrounds them, warm-tinted the way film and tube phosphor both bloom.

05Noise

The coarse blotchy noise that drifts in flat colour — a different animal from film grain, and much more of a giveaway that this is tape.

Faint horizontal banding that shifts down the frame. Not scanlines — dropout and tracking, which is the texture these actually have.

06Frame

The ragged dark border where the picture runs past the tube's usable area.

Left at zero on purpose. Broadcast video on a consumer set had no visible line structure — drawn-on scanlines are what most makes a "VHS filter" look fake.

The torn, skewed strip at the bottom of the frame where the tape head changes over. Genuinely diagnostic of VHS and almost never faked.
